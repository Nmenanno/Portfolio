02 / CONTROL SYSTEMS · FALL 2025CPS / AERE 3640

Altitude control.
From signal to flight.

Our team built a lighter-than-air craft that used an ultrasonic sensor to measure altitude and an RC receiver to set the desired height. A Raspberry Pi adjusted two lift motors, while a third motor provided forward thrust.

We developed the altitude controller from proportional control to proportional–derivative (PD) control, adding damping after early flights revealed oscillations of several feet.

Hardware debugging: correcting inverted UART data

HARDWARE DEBUGGING / UART SIGNAL INVERSION

The sensor was speaking.
The Pi was reading it backwards.

The ultrasonic sensor initially produced unreadable serial data. We connected an oscilloscope and discovered that its TX pulses were inverted relative to what the Raspberry Pi’s UART expected.

Using the transistor NOT-gate approach from Lab 2, we built an inverter between the sensor’s TX pin and the Pi’s serial input. The gate reversed the signal polarity so the Pi could read the altitude data correctly.

Control software and test results

Adding measurable damping

The control loop ran approximately 20 times per second. Adjacent altitude readings did not provide a useful derivative estimate, so we used a queue of the last 20 readings to measure change over a longer interval and add derivative damping.

Recovering serial message boundaries

Timing variations in the main loop could cause invalid reads. The sensor-reading function waited for the leading “R” byte before parsing the distance, rather than blindly taking the next five bytes.

What the tests showed

The report records a satisfactory flight after several attempts. Added damping reduced altitude oscillation, but rotational drift remained. A separate heading-control experiment worked in a hang test; combining it with altitude control caused motor saturation and tilting, so that version was not flight-tested.

Lessons carried forward

Test sensor signals early, account for the testing surface, and plan electronics access. Carpet interfered with the ultrasonic measurements, while limited wiring space made integration harder than it needed to be.

Parametric geometry

My Python geometry module calculates aspect ratio, wing loading, root and tip chords, and mean aerodynamic chord. It provides reusable inputs for stability and performance analysis.

An airfoil database built from analysis

The processed NACA 4409 dataset covers Reynolds numbers of 60,000, 80,000, 100,000, 150,000, and 250,000. The workflow organizes XFLR5 polars and flags missing angle-of-attack points and lift-curve reversals before using the data for screening.

A complete aircraft problem

The design plan considers powered climb, power-to-glide transition, minimum sink, structural weight, stability, and recovery together. Construction, trimming, and flight testing are later project stages.

Research direction and experimental plan

Current stage: scope and simulation

My Fall 2026 work focuses on simulation infrastructure and reviewing decomposition and composition methods. My compute plan includes Isaac Sim / Isaac Lab and Iowa State’s Nova cluster. The annual scope worksheet establishes the working project title; the final research question, testbed, baseline algorithm, and proposed extension remain to be selected with my mentors.

Candidate research directions

The worksheet considers safe reinforcement learning with a run-time assurance layer, offline learning that prioritizes safety, and robust perception-to-control methods. Reward decomposition and recomposition is an additional direction under exploration. These are candidate approaches for narrowing the project scope.

How the work will be evaluated

The planned workflow is to reproduce a baseline, introduce a focused extension, and compare the two under controlled conditions. Candidate measures include mission success, safety violations or collision rate, robustness to changed conditions, and computational cost. Exact metrics and targets will follow the final scope decision.

Academic-year milestones

The fall plan progresses from a reproducible baseline to a pilot comparison and a documented experiment pipeline. Spring targets include broader experiments, ablation and robustness studies, and a final research presentation and code handoff. These are planned deliverables; quantitative results are not yet reported here.

Reproducible experiments

The research plan calls for version-controlled code, recorded software environments, fixed evaluation seeds and scenarios, comparable experiment budgets, and figures regenerated from saved data. Monthly updates and semester reports will document progress and findings.

My primary contribution: motor control & integration · Dec 2024–May 2025

Team objective: a modular robotic arm

The NASA-sponsored spacecraft robotics project explored a modular arm that could be extended with additional sections and attachments. The aim was a flexible architecture that could grow with the task, rather than a fixed-length arm.

My scope: electrical setup, programming, and actuation testing

From December 2024 through May 2025, I assembled the Arduino Uno and breadboard connections and developed the stepper-motor control code in the Arduino IDE. I verified motor rotation during bench testing, then integrated a stepper motor into one arm module and demonstrated actuation of that section.

Debugging control of two motors

The main electrical challenge was establishing the correct pin connections and breadboard routing, particularly when controlling two stepper motors simultaneously. I corrected the wiring configuration and revised the control inputs in the code to resolve the issue and verify operation of the motor setup.

What worked—and what limited the mechanism

Testing verified motor control on the bench and movement within one arm module. Tight clearances in the team’s CAD design produced excessive friction, limiting efficient stepper-driven movement. My demonstrated result was actuation of the tested module; the mechanical friction remained a limitation of the overall arm.

What I learned

This work connected embedded programming, circuit troubleshooting, and mechanical integration. It reinforced the value of verifying motor control before installation, checking pin assignments against the code, and separating electrical control issues from friction in the mechanism.

Pneumatic soft robotics: TPU finger proof of concept · Aug 2025–May 2026

Objective: pneumatic actuation in a printed finger

The second phase of this research explored a soft robotic finger made from 3D-printed TPU, with a simple syringe used for pneumatic input. We designed and printed a finger as a proof of concept.

My contribution: CAD and fabrication

I designed the finger in SolidWorks and printed it in TPU on a Bambu H2D. The work connected mechanical design and additive manufacturing with syringe-based pneumatic integration.

Design challenge: wall thickness and deformation

The main challenge was designing a TPU finger that would contract or expand under pneumatic input. Wall thickness and the finger’s geometry were central to its deformation behavior. Producing the printed proof of concept was a concrete outcome; achieving the intended motion remained the key mechanical-design challenge.

Experimental use of AI

This phase also included experimental use of AI in the mechanical-design process, alongside SolidWorks modeling and physical prototyping.
